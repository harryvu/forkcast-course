Recommendations

Part 3, Language, last lesson. About 2 hours. No GPU needed. You'll leave with a recommender that learns what each customer likes from nothing but their order history, and the metric that tells you whether it beats "show everyone the popular places".

Objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Frame recommendation as learning a vector for every user and every restaurant so that their dot product predicts orders.
  2. Build matrix factorization from two nn.Embedding tables plus biases, and score every restaurant for a batch of users in one matrix product.
  3. Train on implicit feedback with sampled negatives and a binary cross-entropy loss.
  4. Evaluate with leave-one-out hit rate at k, against the popularity baseline.
  5. Check what the model learned against the hidden tastes that generated the data.

Where we are

Forkcast knows what every customer has ordered. Opening the app should show each of them the restaurants they are most likely to order from next, including places they have never seen. There is no text to read and no photo to look at, just a table of (customer, restaurant) pairs, and from that alone a model can learn that people who order from Pho Saigon also like Ramen Lab. The mechanism is the embedding table once more, this time with two of them, and the dot product between a user's row and a restaurant's row as the prediction. The orders here are synthetic and generated from hidden taste vectors, so at the end you can check what the model recovered.

Tastes as vectors

Give every user a vector and every restaurant a vector in the same space, and let the score for a pair be their dot product plus a bias for the user (orders a lot), a bias for the restaurant (popular) and one global offset. Training moves the vectors so that pairs that happened score high and pairs that did not score low. Nobody tells the model what the dimensions mean, but after training they are tastes: one direction might separate spicy from mild, another light from hearty, and a user's vector points toward what they order. This is matrix factorization, and a user's recommendations are the restaurants their vector points at that they have not tried.

class MatrixFactorization(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_users, n_items, dim=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.user_vec, self.item_vec = nn.Embedding(n_users, dim), nn.Embedding(n_items, dim)
        self.user_bias, self.item_bias = nn.Embedding(n_users, 1), nn.Embedding(n_items, 1)
        self.global_bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))
        for table in (self.user_vec, self.item_vec):
            nn.init.normal_(table.weight, std=0.05)                   # small start: early scores near zero
    def forward(self, users, items):                                   # (N,), (N,) -> (N,)
        dot = (self.user_vec(users) * self.item_vec(items)).sum(dim=1)
        return dot + self.user_bias(users).squeeze(1) + self.item_bias(items).squeeze(1) + self.global_bias
    def score_all(self, users):                                        # (N,) -> (N, n_items): every restaurant at once
        return self.user_vec(users) @ self.item_vec.weight.T + self.user_bias(users) + self.item_bias.weight.T + self.global_bias

count_parameters(MatrixFactorization(300, 120))      7141   300×16 + 120×16 + 300 + 120 + 1

score_all is Lesson 2's X @ w grown up: a batch of user vectors times the whole restaurant table gives every score in one matrix product, which is what makes ranking the entire catalogue for a million users feasible.

Implicit feedback and negatives

The order table only records what happened. There is no row saying "customer 42 would hate Burger Barn", only silence, and silence mostly means they never saw it. Training therefore uses each real order as a positive example and, for each one, draws a random restaurant the customer has not ordered from as a weak negative. The loss is binary cross-entropy with logits: push the positive score toward "yes", the negative toward "no". Some negatives are wrong, a place they would love, but with thousands of orders the real signal wins.

for users, positives in loader:                      # the (user, item) pairs that happened
    negatives = sample_negatives(users, n_items, seen)   # one unordered restaurant per positive
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(model(users, positives), torch.ones(len(users))) + \
           F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(model(users, negatives), torch.zeros(len(users)))
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()

epoch losses: 1.369, 1.159, 0.738, 0.569, 0.497, 0.432   (every second epoch of twelve)

Ranking metrics: hit rate at k

Accuracy means nothing here; the model never says yes or no, it ranks. The honest test is Lesson 5's split per user: hide each customer's most recent order, rank every restaurant they have not tried, and ask whether the hidden one is in the top k. The fraction of users for whom it is, is hit rate at k. It has a floor, random guessing gives about k over the number of unseen restaurants, and a baseline that every recommender must beat before it is worth deploying: rank by popularity alone.

frame, user_factors, item_factors = make_order_history()   # 300 users × 20 distinct restaurants, 120 restaurants
train, test = leave_one_out(frame)                   5700 training orders, 300 held-out ones, one per user
seen = seen_items(train)

popularity_hit_rate(train, test, seen, k=10)         0.103   barely above random (10 of 100 unseen restaurants)
hit_rate_at_k(untrained_model, test, seen, k=10)     0.080
hit_rate_at_k(trained_model, test, seen, k=10)       0.557   after twelve epochs; 0.377 at k=5, 0.770 at k=20

What the model learned

Because the orders were generated from hidden taste vectors, you can compare. Normalise the learned restaurant vectors, compute every pairwise cosine similarity, do the same for the hidden factors, and correlate the two matrices: 0.88 in the sandbox. The model was shown nothing but order pairs and reconstructed the geometry that produced them. The same comparison on a real system would be your product team's opinion of which restaurants are alike; the model's answer is usually close and occasionally surprising.

  7. Recommend for one user

    Pick a user, list what they ordered, mask those restaurants with -inf and print the top five, then the held-out item. Do it for a few users; some will be hits, some near misses at rank 12, and that spread is what hit rate summarises.

  8. Break it on purpose: forget to mask the seen items

    Evaluate hit_rate_at_k without the -inf masking. The top of every list fills with restaurants the user already ordered from, the held-out item is pushed down, and the metric collapses, even though the model is unchanged. A recommender that re-recommends what you just bought is the most common bug in the field, and it is a two-line fix.

Common errors

Hit rate is near zero and never improves
The test items are also in the training set and you mask seen items, so the answer is hidden from the ranking; or the split was not per user. Leave one order out per user and keep it out of seen.
Hit rate collapses after a few epochs while the loss still falls
Overfitting the item biases or the vectors: add weight_decay, lower dim, or stop early on the hit rate rather than the loss.
RuntimeError: The size of tensor a (64) must match the size of tensor b (120)
The biases in score_all are the wrong way round: the user bias is (N, 1), the item bias must be (1, n_items), which is item_bias.weight.T.
Every user gets the same five recommendations
The item bias has taken over and the vectors are tiny: too much weight decay, or a learning rate too low to move the vectors. Also check the negatives are per user; sampling the same negatives for everyone teaches nothing personal.
sample_negatives returns restaurants the user ordered from
The seen sets were built from the frame after the split, or keyed by the wrong column. seen_items(train), keyed by user.
Loss sits at 1.386 and does not move
Two BCE terms at chance: 2 × ln 2. The vectors were initialised at zero or the optimizer is not updating the embedding tables; check model.parameters() was passed, not a copy.

Stretch

Replace the BCE pair loss with a ranking loss: for each positive and negative, -F.logsigmoid(score_pos - score_neg), which asks only that the ordered restaurant score higher than the sampled one. Compare hit rate.
